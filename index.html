Visible Doesn't Mean Usable.

An accessibility feature can exist, meet a checklist, and still fail the people who need it. AccessCheck KZ asks three questions of every photo instead, and proposes using AI to help ask them across the whole country.

The problem

About 752,000 people in Kazakhstan are registered as having a disability, 3.7% of the population.[1] Whether they can enter a clinic, a school or a pharmacy depends on a few metres of concrete at the door.

What the United Nations found

Kazakhstan ratified the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities in 2015.[3] In its first review, in March 2024, the UN committee raised three concerns that this project builds on:[4]

  1. Narrow requirementsAccessibility requirements are narrow and centred on physical items such as ramps, with little information on schools, housing, clinics and shops, especially in rural areas.
  2. No binding enforcementNo legally binding mechanism enforces accessibility standards in public or private buildings.
  3. Under-countingThe medical model used for registration leads to people with disabilities being under-counted in national statistics.

Why photographs

In field monitoring reported by CABAR, pharmacies and clinics failed for ordinary reasons.[3] Each of these is visible in a photograph, which is why photographs are the starting point here.

Limits of the available data

1

Registration is not prevalence

The 3.7% figure counts people registered through medical and social assessment. The UN committee says this approach under-represents people with disabilities.[4] The World Health Organization estimates that about 16% of people worldwide have a significant disability, measured differently.[10]

2

"Adapted" is not defined the same way twice

The official map records certified equipment. Civil-society maps rate whether a person can get in and use the service. In Almaty, 10,700 facilities are listed as fully or partly adapted, yet only 1,600 are adapted for wheelchair users.[9]

3

Figures come from different dates

The numbers here span 2017 to 2026 and several were reported by news outlets quoting the ministry's portal, not read from the portal directly. Check each against the primary source before citing it.

4

The map covers a fraction of buildings

About 43,000 administrative and social facilities are registered.[1] Sidewalks, crossings, housing and most private businesses are outside it.

Current research

Where researchers disagree

Law on paper or change on the ground

Kazakhstan's legislation is largely consistent with the UN convention, Koishibayev and colleagues find.[22]

vs

There are no effective mechanisms to carry it out.[22] The UN committee's 2024 review makes a similar point about the lack of binding enforcement of accessibility standards.[4]

Official definitions or lived experience

The built-environment studies judge accessibility against building standards and comfort measures.[13][25]

vs

Allan and Omarova show that official systems of classification can miss what disabled people actually need.[23]

Satisfactory for pedestrians, or accessible for everyone

Tolegen and colleagues rate pedestrian access in an Almaty district as satisfactory overall, while listing missing ramps among its faults.[25]

vs

Abdrassilova and Murzagaliyeva, looking at the same city from the position of low-mobility groups, conclude that people with disabilities generally cannot move around safely without help.[13]

People, machines, or both

People. Saha and colleagues rely on volunteers and paid workers to label street imagery.[11]

Machines. Moreira and colleagues automate detection.[24]

Both. Hara and colleagues combine the two, letting computer vision propose and people verify.[12]

Gaps this project responds to

  1. The three topics rarely meet. The Kazakhstan sources deal with law, education policy and architecture. The assessment tools were developed and tested in North America and Portugal.[11][12][24]
  2. Compliance is named as the problem but not measured at scale. Ramps that fail the regulations are reported for Kazakhstan in the policy source and in both Almaty studies.[22][13][25] The detection tools identify whether a ramp is present or missing, not whether it complies.[12][24]
  3. Evidence stops at one city. Both built-environment studies rely on manual field surveys in Almaty, one of them in part of a single district.[13][25] Nothing in the set covers other regions or rural areas.
  4. Sidewalks are studied, entrances are not. The three technical studies work on sidewalks and curb ramps seen from the street.[11][12][24] None assesses building entrances.

Overall research contribution

Together, these studies establish three foundations for AccessCheck KZ.

1Research on disability rights reveals gaps between policy and implementation in Kazakhstan.

2Urban infrastructure studies document physical barriers and limitations of existing assessments.

3International research demonstrates how AI and public participation can support accessibility mapping.

However, no study among these seven evaluates a system that combines AI-based visual detection, standards compliance, and disabled people's assessments of functional usability in Kazakhstan.

AccessCheck KZ proposes to address this gap through a three-level assessment framework that distinguishes between what AI can see, what regulations require, and what people can actually use.

AI solution: the AccessCheck KZ prototype

The proposed system reads a photo of an entrance, ramp or sidewalk and reports at three levels. Each level is harder than the last, and each needs a person to confirm it.

      How the full system would work

      1. Photo Submission

        A resident, inspector or organisation of people with disabilities submits a photo with a location.

      2. AI Detection

        A detector trained on local images proposes level 1 features and flags possible level 2 and 3 problems, with a confidence score.

      3. Human Verification

        A trained reviewer accepts, corrects or rejects every suggestion. Reviewers include wheelchair users and other people with lived experience.

      4. Accessibility Map

        Confirmed results are published beside the official status so the two can be compared.

      How the three levels are defined
      1. Visual presence. Is a ramp, step, door, handrail or obstacle visible? This is object detection, the task existing research does best.
      2. Standards compliance. Do slope, width and threshold meet Kazakhstan's building rules (SN RK 3.06-01-2011 and SP RK 3.06-101-2012)?[18] A single photo rarely gives reliable measurements.
      3. Functional usability. Could a person get from the street through the door, today, without help? This depends on parked cars, snow, a locked gate, a heavy door.

      Six kinds of cost

      EnvironmentalSmall per image, on a fossil-heavy grid

      A task-specific detector uses very little electricity per image: 0.038 kWh per thousand in the reference study, against 2.9 kWh per thousand for image generation.[14] Kazakhstan's grid is about 85% fossil-fuelled, at roughly 600 g CO₂e per kWh, so the same work emits more here than on a cleaner grid.[15] The practical rule is to use the smallest model that does the job.

      EconomicPeople cost more than computing

      Computing is the small line. Human review dominates at any realistic volume. For scale, planned public spending on support for people with disabilities in 2026 is 1.1 trillion tenge.[1] Finding a barrier does not pay for removing it.

      LaborLabelling and checking is skilled work

      Someone has to label the training photos and check the results. Project Sidewalk needed 797 volunteers and paid workers over 18 months to place about 205,000 labels in one city.[11] This is skilled work, and people with disabilities should be paid to do and lead it, not asked to volunteer.

      GovernanceIt needs an owner and an appeals route

      Kazakhstan's Law on Artificial Intelligence took effect on 18 January 2026. It sorts systems into three risk levels and places the duty to classify on the system's owner.[19] A tool that influences which buildings are certified would need an owner, an appeals route and published error rates. The UN committee already notes the lack of binding enforcement.[4]

      Data ethicsPhotos capture faces, plates and homes

      Street photos capture faces, number plates and homes, which fall under the Law on Personal Data and their Protection.[20] Photos should be blurred before storage and kept only as long as needed.

      AccuracyAbout one detection in seven is wrong

      A wrong "accessible" label sends someone to a door they cannot enter. Even a strong detector, at 85% precision for curb ramps, is wrong about roughly one detection in seven, and scanning whole panoramas lowered overall precision further.[24] Unreviewed output would mislead.

      Final verdict

      Should Kazakhstan implement AccessCheck KZ?

      Yes — but AI should support human inspections, not replace them.

      AccessCheck KZ could help make accessibility problems more visible to government authorities and the public. Instead of simply recording whether a building has a ramp or another accessibility feature, the system would help identify barriers that need further inspection and verification against accessibility standards.

      By analyzing photographs, AI could reduce the time required to identify potential problems and support data collection across Kazakhstan, including both urban and rural areas.

      Benefits

      • Better accountabilityAuthorities could identify reported barriers, track whether they have been addressed, and verify that improvements meet accessibility standards.
      • Faster assessmentsAI could help screen large numbers of photographs, allowing inspectors to focus on locations requiring attention.
      • Wider coverageCommunity submissions could help collect accessibility information from cities, smaller towns, and rural communities.
      • Meaningful accessibilityThe focus would shift from simply installing ramps to ensuring that infrastructure is compliant and genuinely usable.

      Limitations

      • Incomplete photographsImages may not show the entire entrance, ramp, or surrounding environment. AI may overlook important barriers or interpret features incorrectly.
      • Standardized photo collectionContributors would need clear instructions about which angles, distances, and features to photograph to make assessments more reliable.
      • Human verificationPhotographs alone cannot establish exact dimensions, compliance with regulations, or usability for every person. Trained inspectors and people with disabilities must verify the results.
      • Unequal participationRural areas and communities with limited internet access may remain underrepresented without targeted outreach.

      Conditions for implementation

      1. PilotStart with a pilot in one urban and one rural area to evaluate accuracy and usefulness.
      2. Photo guidelinesDevelop clear photography guidelines with accessibility experts and organizations representing people with disabilities.
      3. Screen, then inspectUse AI for preliminary screening, followed by measurements, inspections, and community assessments where necessary.
      4. AccountabilityEstablish government accountability through a transparent process for reporting, addressing, and verifying accessibility problems.
      5. Data protectionProtect personal data by obtaining appropriate consent and removing identifying information from submitted photographs.
      6. EvaluateEvaluate the pilot based on detection accuracy, geographic coverage, costs, and whether reported barriers are actually resolved.

      The bottom line

      AccessCheck KZ should be tested as a tool that helps Kazakhstan move from counting accessibility features to verifying real accessibility.

      Its success should not be measured by how many ramps AI detects, but by how many barriers are identified, verified, and ultimately removed.

      The goal is not to count more ramps. It is to make sure people can actually use them.

      Rejected because photographs alone cannot establish compliance with building standards or functional usability.

      Rejected because AI can make mistakes. Human inspection and assessments by people with disabilities remain essential.

      Rejected because official figures and civil-society assessments use different definitions, samples, and methods.
