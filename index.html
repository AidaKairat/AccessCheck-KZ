A ramp on the map is not a way into the building.

Kazakhstan counts a facility as adapted when the equipment is there. AccessCheck KZ asks three questions of every photo instead, and proposes using AI to help ask them across the whole country.

The problem

About 752,000 people in Kazakhstan are registered as having a disability, 3.7% of the population.[1] Whether they can enter a clinic, a school or a pharmacy depends on a few metres of concrete at the door.

Two accounts of the same buildings

The state and civil society describe accessibility in Kazakhstan very differently. Both accounts are reported below as their sources give them. They use different definitions, samples and years, so they are not a like-for-like comparison. The size of the gap is the point.

What the United Nations found

Kazakhstan ratified the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities in 2015.[3] In its first review, in March 2024, the UN committee raised three concerns that this project builds on:[4]

  • Accessibility requirements are narrow and centred on physical items such as ramps, with little information on schools, housing, clinics and shops, especially in rural areas.
  • No legally binding mechanism enforces accessibility standards in public or private buildings.
  • The medical model used for registration leads to people with disabilities being under-counted in national statistics.

In field monitoring reported by CABAR, pharmacies and clinics failed for ordinary reasons: no ramp, a ramp too steep to use, a narrow door, or a raised threshold.[3] Each of these is visible in a photograph, which is why photographs are the starting point here.

Limits of the available data

Registration is not prevalence

The 3.7% figure counts people registered through medical and social assessment. The UN committee says this approach under-represents people with disabilities.[4] The World Health Organization estimates that about 16% of people worldwide have a significant disability, measured differently.[11]

"Adapted" is not defined the same way twice

The official map records certified equipment. Civil-society maps rate whether a person can get in and use the service. In Almaty, 10,700 facilities are listed as fully or partly adapted, yet only 1,600 are adapted for wheelchair users.[10]

Figures come from different dates

The numbers here span 2017 to 2026 and several were reported by news outlets quoting the ministry's portal, not read from the portal directly. Check each against the primary source before citing it.

The map covers a fraction of buildings

About 43,000 administrative and social facilities are registered.[1] Sidewalks, crossings, housing and most private businesses are outside it.

Where researchers disagree

Scale or accuracy
Automation covers more streets than volunteers can. But the best automated curb-ramp detector in this set was right about one detection in three (33.7% precision at 78.7% recall), and for missing curb ramps one in eight.[14] Crowd workers reached about 68% on both measures.[12]
Detecting a feature or judging its condition
Liu and colleagues note that most models find a ramp but do not say whether it is usable, and their own fine-grained model reached a reliable score on only 13 of 33 condition tags.[15]
More data or cleaner data
Training on a larger but noisier label set cut curb-ramp tag performance roughly in half in the same study.[15] Cleaning took four assistants more than 100 hours.
Fairness or justice
Bennett and Keyes argue that computer vision built "for" disabled people can entrench existing gatekeepers and legitimise surveillance unless disabled people hold power over its design.[17]

Gaps this project responds to

  • No Kazakhstan evidence. None of the technical studies reviewed here reports results on imagery from Kazakhstan or Central Asia. Project Sidewalk's label types follow United States standards and its authors say they may need adapting elsewhere.[12]
  • Entrances are under-studied. The technical work concentrates on sidewalks and curb ramps seen from the street. The Kazakhstan source treats entrances and buildings as an architectural question, without measurement at scale.[16]
  • Compliance is not measured. None of the six sources tests whether a photo can establish that a ramp meets a national building standard.
  • Severity and usability are left out. Severity ratings are explicitly not inferred in the most recent benchmark.[15]

AI solution: the AccessCheck KZ prototype

The proposed system reads a photo of an entrance, ramp or sidewalk and reports at three levels. Each level is harder than the last, and each needs a person to confirm it.

  1. Visual presence

    Is a ramp, step, door, handrail or obstacle visible? This is object detection, the task existing research does best.

  2. Standards compliance

    Do slope, width and threshold meet Kazakhstan's building rules (SN RK 3.06-01-2011 and SP RK 3.06-101-2012)?[22] A single photo rarely gives reliable measurements.

  3. Functional usability

    Could a person get from the street through the door, today, without help? This depends on parked cars, snow, a locked gate, a heavy door.

How the full system would work

  1. Photo in. A resident, inspector or organisation of people with disabilities submits a photo with a location.
  2. Model suggests. A detector trained on local images proposes level 1 features and flags possible level 2 and 3 problems, with a confidence score.
  3. Person confirms. A trained reviewer accepts, corrects or rejects every suggestion. Reviewers include wheelchair users and other people with lived experience.
  4. Map updates. Confirmed results are published beside the official status so the two can be compared.

Environmental

A task-specific detector uses very little electricity per image: 0.038 kWh per thousand in the reference study, against 2.9 kWh per thousand for image generation.[18] Kazakhstan's grid is about 85% fossil-fuelled, at roughly 600 g CO₂e per kWh, so the same work emits more here than on a cleaner grid.[19] The practical rule is to use the smallest model that does the job.

Economic

Computing is the small line. Human review dominates at any realistic volume. For scale, planned public spending on support for people with disabilities in 2026 is 1.1 trillion tenge.[1] Finding a barrier does not pay for removing it.

Labour

Someone has to label the training photos and check the results. Cleaning 24,000 labels took four research assistants more than 100 hours in one study.[15] This is skilled work, and people with disabilities should be paid to do and lead it, not asked to volunteer.

Governance

Kazakhstan's Law on Artificial Intelligence took effect on 18 January 2026. It sorts systems into three risk levels and places the duty to classify on the system's owner.[23] A tool that influences which buildings are certified would need an owner, an appeals route and published error rates. The UN committee already notes the lack of binding enforcement.[4]

Data ethics

Street photos capture faces, number plates and homes, which fall under the Law on Personal Data and their Protection.[24] Photos should be blurred before storage and kept only as long as needed. Bennett and Keyes warn that assistive computer vision can normalise surveillance.[17]

Accuracy as a cost

A wrong "accessible" label sends someone to a door they cannot enter. With detector precision as low as 12% for missing ramps in published work, unreviewed output would do harm.[14]

Final verdict

Should Kazakhstan implement this system?

Yes, as a supervised pilot that helps people audit faster. No, as an automatic certifier of buildings.

Why a pilot is justified

  • The official and civil-society pictures of accessibility are far apart, and photos are evidence both sides can inspect.[1][3]
  • The most common failures seen in monitoring are visible in a photo.[3]
  • Combining a model with human checking has matched manual quality while saving reviewer time in earlier work.[13]
  • Running costs are small next to existing public spending.

Why automation alone is not

  • Published accuracy is too low for unreviewed decisions.[14]
  • No model has been tested on Kazakhstan's streets, buildings or winters.
  • Levels 2 and 3, which matter most to users, are the least solved.[15]
  • Without binding enforcement, better detection changes little.[4]

Conditions for going ahead

  1. Start in one city and one rural district, with a public evaluation against on-site audits.
  2. Organisations of people with disabilities co-design the labels and hold a veto over publication.
  3. Every published result is confirmed by a trained, paid reviewer.
  4. Error rates are published by level, region and season.
  5. Photos are blurred and stored under the personal data law, with a named owner under the AI law.
  6. Results appear beside the official map status, with a route to dispute either.
